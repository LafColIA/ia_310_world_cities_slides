A working definition of a city (Cowgill, 2004)

  • A permanent settlement, home to a significant number of residents
  • Population above a few thousand
  • Urban and rural identities that differ
10

All settlements have catchment areas, but only cities have hinterlands. Inhabitants of cities may have interests and even additional dwellings in the countryside, and rural people may visit cities for many purposes — the distinction is, above all, one of identities.

— Cowgill, 2004

Threshold: over the bar, still a village (Jennings & Earle, 2016)

  • Çatalhöyük held perhaps 8,000 residents
    • Çatalhöyük's own excavator calls the settlement "just a very very large village"
      • village egalitarianism pushed "to its ultimate extreme"

16

Hinterland: no separate countryside

  • Angkor and the Maya lowlands spread over hundreds of square kilometres
  • Densities as low as 1.6–2.8 people per hectare at Great Zimbabwe (Fletcher, 2020)
  • Fields, shrines and dwellings interpenetrate inside the urban area itself (Fletcher, 2020)
17

Permanence: seasonal and mobile settlements

["The Town of Leetakoo" (Litakun). Samuel Daniell aquatint, 1804–05. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.]{.caption}

  • Mongol ordus and Mughal encampments administered while mobile (Fletcher, 2020)
  • Tswana towns averaged 15 years in one place, production dispersed to cattle posts (Chirikure, 2020)
  • Zulu capitals of grass and timber rebuilt with each reign (Chirikure, 2020)

18

Monumentality and cityhood

[The Qorikancha, the Inca Temple of the Sun, Cuzco. Håkan Svensson (Xauxa) / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.5.]{.caption}

  • Inca Cuzco: no commoners and no market, a city by form and a ceremonial centre by function (Rowe, 1967)
  • Yoruba towns: 50,000-plus heterogeneous people, no monumental architecture (Chirikure, 2020)
  • Monuments neither guarantee nor are required for urban life

19

A Eurocentric and culturally inflexible application of Childe's traits prompted some to conclude, rather naively, that Africa south of the Sahara had no cities or civilizations.

— Chirikure, 2020

Alternatives to a yes/no test

  • Cowgill (2004): abandon the yes/no question, measure along every axis at once
  • Fletcher (2020): track trajectories of growth and demise instead of static types
  • Edwards (2024): an unbroken sequence from home-base to megacity

What to take away

  • No agreed definition of "city" exists (Cowgill, 2004; Fletcher, 2020)
  • Every criterion breaks somewhere: four settlements, four different failures
  • Which places count depends on the yardstick
