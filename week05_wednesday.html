The Cloaca Maxima was not a sewer (Hopkins, 2007)

  • The Cloaca began as a monumental open-air fresh-water canal
  • Contemporary drainage canals were three times larger
  • Same stone, block size and technique as the Capitoline Temple
09

[Al-Māwardī] also asserts that a ruler's first concern when founding a city (miṣr) should be the availability of fresh water. According to him, the ruler must uphold eight conditions concerning the rights of a city's inhabitants (ḥuqūq sākinīh), the first of which is bringing water to the city, either via running canals (anhār jāriya) or, if necessary, by constructing water-filled cisterns (ḥiyāḍ sāʾila).

— van den Bent, 2025

Al-Māwardī: an Abbasid-era Sunni jurist (d. 450/1058) whose manuals on governance and taxation, among them Al-Aḥkām al-Sulṭāniyya, set out what a ruler owed a city's inhabitants (van den Bent, 2025).

Half a million people on 150 mm of rain (van den Bent, 2025)

  • Baghdad gets about 150 mm of rain a year
  • Baghdad nonetheless held hundreds of thousands, perhaps 500,000
  • A qanāt from the Karkhāyā Canal was built with the city, in 762

The Round City between the Tigris and the Sarat and Abu Attab canals. Le Strange, Baghdad During the Abbasid Caliphate, 1900, Map II. Public domain. Set on white for legibility: the plate's ink is black.

Ten cities, two answers

13

Tikal's centralized reservoirs (Chase & Cesaretti, 2019)

  • Large monumental reservoirs, connected to the urban core
  • One central network, drawn on by the whole city
  • Fits elite-control models of water access

Grand Plaza and Temple I, Tikal. Greg Willis, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 2.0.

14

Caracol's household reservoirs (Chase & Cesaretti, 2019)

  • Thousands of small residential reservoirs, built and held per household
  • Storage sits beside houses instead of in the civic core
  • Complicates elite-control accounts of Maya water

From the top of Caana, Caracol, Belize. R Barraez D'Lucca, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0.

15

Terrain conditioned the choice, not a shared template (Chase & Cesaretti, 2019)

  • Caracol: rugged karst, mean slope 8.2°
  • Tikal: gentler ground, mean slope 4.1°
  • Both cities changed arrangements over time; terrain set no fixed template

Geomorphon shares from 30 m SRTM data (Chase & Cesaretti, 2019).

16

Or you buy water from a carrier (Brown & van Berkel, 2024)

  • Samarra's salty wells meant drinking water came in from the Tigris
  • Excavated Samarra houses show no connection to any citywide network
  • Fusṭāṭ houses have niches built to receive a carrier's delivery
17

Chang'an: one network, unequal service (Chen et al., 2022)

  • Private wells served wealthy households and the imperial family
  • Ordinary residents used public ward wells and canal surface water
  • More than 40 wells documented, about half in the southeast

Tang Chang'an. LackOfInspiration1, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0. Set on white for legibility: the map's labels are black.

Who pays for upkeep? (van den Bent, 2025)

  • Caliphal involvement concentrated in new foundations
  • Maintenance in practice was far more haphazard than the norm
  • Baghdad's teakwood conduits stop when the court leaves

What to take away

  • Water infrastructure is prestige as much as plumbing: the Cloaca in cut stone (Hopkins, 2007)
  • Distributive or collective supply sorts people differently: Caracol's household tanks against Tikal's central ones (Chase & Cesaretti, 2019)
  • Funding new waterworks was hard; funding upkeep was harder: Samarra's canal abandoned unfinished (van den Bent, 2025)
