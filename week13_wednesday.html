Ninety days by keelboat, four by steamboat

Thomas M. Easterly, "St. Louis Levee, Steamboat Illinois," 1852. Missouri History Museum, public domain.

11

Fur, lead, and Santa Fe silver (Melom, 1947)

  • Fur, 1815–1830: $2.1m spent against $3.8m returned
  • Lead worth roughly $1.0m a year by 1821
  • Two-fifths of Upper Mississippi steamboat tonnage was lead
  • Santa Fe silver, $100,000–$200,000 a year from the mid-1820s

Three decades of bank failures

Melom (1947).

14

Barely industrial until the 1840s (Melom, 1947)

  • Manufacturing at $200,000 against $3.9m in retail, 1840
  • Flour milling: 2 mills in 1840, 15 by 1845
  • Output rises from 14,000 barrels a year to 2.1 million
  • Nine St. Louis packers took 30,000 hogs; one Alton firm took 50,000
15

Wage-earners as a class (Melom, 1947)

  • First strike: journeymen tailors, December 1835
  • Bricklayers' pay fell from $5.00 to $3.50 per thousand bricks
  • Most mechanics took a 50–75% wage cut in the 1837 depression
  • The Mechanics' and Workingmen's Party moved 1,000 votes in 1840
16

Keeping the levee open (Melom, 1947)

  • Sandbar removal estimated at $41,000 in 1834 (about $1.6M in 2025 dollars)
  • The 1834 federal appropriation, $15,000 (about $580,000 in 2025 dollars)
  • Bloody Island dikes built under Robert E. Lee, 1837–1842

20

At St. Louis the barrier effects of the Mississippi River during the first 25 years of the railroad era in the Middle West were disastrous to the commercial prestige of St. Louis. During those years, the railroads literally dumped their shipments on the eastern bank of the river and told the merchants of St. Louis to come and get them.

— Thomas, 1949

21

The Eads Bridge, 1874 (Thomas, 1949)

Compton & Co., St. Louis, c. 1874. U.S. Library of Congress, Prints and Photographs Division, ppmsca.08973. Public domain.

  • Roughly 2,000 ferries worked the crossing before a bridge existed
  • The barrier held about 25 years; the Eads Bridge opened 1874
  • Terminal Railroad Association tolls, about $24m a year by 1924

22

Structure, or choice?

  • Thomas: freight geometry, a river's width, a routing decision in Washington
  • Bourgois: a merchant elite committed to river trade and to plantation society
  • The mercantile elite declined railroad investment while Chicago drew eastern capital
  • The corpus does not adjudicate between the two readings

What to take away

  • St. Louis grew with the steamboat; the city's banks kept failing until 1837 (Melom, 1947)
  • With rails, the Midwest became Chicago's hinterland, not St. Louis's: a 32% freight premium in 1854 (Thomas, 1949)
