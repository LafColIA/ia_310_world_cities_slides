A capital ordered into existence

07

Ieyasu picks a branch castle

  • Headquarters moved to Edo in 1590 (Rozman, 1974)
  • A site with a branch castle and an insignificant market
  • Merchants encouraged in behind him for two decades (Totman, 1987)
08

Seventeenth-century Edo was a city built by and for the shōgun. The immense concentration of power in the shōgun imposed stability in the city, and this made it unnecessary for the authorities to interfere directly in the city's general administration and economic affairs.

— Katō, 2000

09

The castle as a levy on the country

Ote-mon gate, Edo Castle. Don Ramey Logan, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0.

  • Capital built by mobilizing goods and labour nationally (Katō, 2000)
  • Outer moat 15.7 km; spiral system finished 1636 (Coaldrake, 1981)
  • Edo Castle in 1606 took 43,000 pieces of shingling wood (Totman, 1987)

10

From command levy to lumber market (Totman, 1987)

  • A boom phase of lumbering from 1580 to 1660
  • A maintenance phase followed after 1660 everywhere except Edo
  • About 125 wholesalers in three guilds organised the trade by the 1700s
11

Flood works on the eastern lowlands (Suzuki, 2022)

Utagawa Hiroshige, Ichikokubashi Bridge in Edo. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

  • Flood works funded on the same levy logic as the castle
  • Daimyō funded the works in exchange for rights to fief tax revenue
  • Commoner firefighting brigades exceeded 10,000 members by the mid-1700s

12

A city that had to be refilled (Rozman, 1974)

  • Net in-migration ran near 10,000 a year in the early 1600s
  • Rising to about 18,000 a year by the early 1700s
  • The sex ratio was roughly two men to one woman in the 1720s
13

Three metropolises, one country (Katō, 2000)

The Gokaidō, the five routes joining Edo to the provinces: the Tōkaidō is route 1, Edo to Kyoto. Artanisen, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.

  • Edo political, Osaka commercial, Kyoto cultural
  • First recorded Edo to Osaka cargo shipping in 1616
  • The Ten Groups of Wholesalers formally established by 1694

Rank, written into the ground

15

Segregation by order, not by market

  • Samurai, clergy and chōnin lived in separate quarters (Katō, 2000)
  • Each order governed under a separate ruling system (Katō, 2000)
  • Fudai daimyō northeast of the castle, tozama southwest (Coaldrake, 1981)

Chōnin (町人): the townspeople, meaning the merchants and artisans as distinct from warriors and clergy. Fudai daimyō: a lord of a house allied to the Tokugawa from before their victory. Tozama: an "outside" lord, of a house that submitted only after the Tokugawa victory.

16

Unequal land shares (Ichikawa, 1994)

Land shares rounded to 10 per cent. Chōninchi density reached about 67,000 per square kilometre.

Chōninchi (町人地): townsmen's land, the wards of the city set aside for the chōnin, as against the samurai and temple wards.

17

Who governed the Chōnin (Katō, 2000)

One ladder of four tiers, over the chōnin alone; the samurai and the clergy were governed under separate systems.

Chō: a city block, and the unit an Edo ward headman was assigned in multiples of. Nanushi: the headman of a chō, a townsman himself, answerable upward for the block's registers and order. Machi-doshiyori: literally "town elder", the standing office between the headmen and the magistrates.

18

Ranks inside the townsmen's order (Katō, 2000)

  • Householders (ie-mochi) at the top of the chōnin order
  • Landlords (ie-nushi) below the householders
  • Tenants on the frontage (omote-tanagari)
  • Tenants in the alleys (ura-tanagari)
  • Tenants excluded from administration entirely
19

A rear tenement, drawn to scale (Saygi, 2026)

Twenty-seven such units filled one block's interior at Honshirogane-chō 2-chōme, none of them with direct street access. Car dimensions from Toyota's published specification.

20

The Meireki fire (Ichikawa, 1994)

*Musashi Abumi*, 1661, the illustrated account of the fire. British Museum copy, via Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

  • The 1657 fire burned 60% of the urban area
  • Nihonbashi Street widened to 18 m against a typical 3.6 to 7.2
  • The start of 260 years of shogunal city-building

21

Building rules by rank, 1613 to 1727 (Coaldrake, 1981)

Regulation of gateways, roofing and building form.

Daimyō: a great territorial lord, holding a domain under the shōgun. Shoin-zukuri: the formal style of Japanese building organised around a reception room with a built-in writing alcove, staggered shelves and a decorative alcove.

How a million people lived

23

Probably the largest city in the world

Edo's series and Naitō Akira's revised peak (Rozman, 1974); London and Paris (Ichikawa, 1994).

24

Edo's water against London's (Hanley, 1987)

  • The Kanda system ran over 41 miles with 3,663 distribution ducts
  • The Tama system, begun 1652, ran over 50 miles
  • Continuous supply, against London's 7 hours a day, 3 days a week

Piped supply, converted to hours per week from both sources' own statements.

25

Sanitation as behaviour (Hanley, 1987)

Utagawa Hiroshige, Sudden Shower over Shin-Ōhashi Bridge and Atake. Metropolitan Museum of Art, via Wikimedia Commons, CC0.

  • Roughly 600 public baths operated in Edo by the early 1800s
  • Leicester had almost 3,000 uncovered cesspits by the mid-1800s
  • Manhattan had an estimated 30,000 cesspools in 1849

26

The several hundred towns and cities of early modern Japan were wooden creatures with ravenous appetites for timber from the archipelago's convoluted mountain valleys.

— Totman, 1987

27

Rice, fuel, and pickled daikon (Ehara, 2010)

  • Adults ate 560 to 630 grams of rice a day, about 80% of energy
  • A carpenter's household spent 22% of income on rice and 7.6% on rent
  • Rent ran 900 to 1,500 mon a month centrally against 120 to 150 outside
  • Pickled daikon cost 16 mon, about double the price of fresh vegetables
